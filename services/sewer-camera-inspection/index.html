What a sewer camera inspection actually shows about a line

A waterproof camera on a flexible cable runs the length of the line, showing cracks, root intrusion, bellies (low spots where the pipe has sagged), offset joints, and material buildup directly on a monitor as it goes. This replaces guessing at what's wrong based on symptoms alone, which matters since a recurring clog, a slow drain, and a collapsed line all present differently on the surface but require very different fixes.

The recording also documents the line's exact condition at a point in time, useful both for planning a repair and, during a home purchase, for knowing what's being inherited before closing.

When a camera inspection is worth doing on its own

Beyond diagnosing an active problem, a standalone inspection makes sense before buying a home with an older sewer line, before recurring clogs get treated as isolated events, and before jetting or trenchless work to confirm the pipe's actual condition first. For older homes in this coverage area with original clay or cast-iron drain lines, a one-time inspection is often the cheapest way to know what's actually likely coming.

What we specifically look for during an inspection

Beyond an obvious blockage, we check for pipe material and transitions (a common failure point where clay meets cast iron, for instance), the slope and grade along the run, root intrusion at joints, and any bellying where the pipe has settled unevenly. Each of these points toward a different repair approach, so the inspection report notes the specific finding and its location, not just a pass or fail.

For an older home, we also note the overall material of the line, clay, cast iron, or original Orangeburg pipe in rare cases, since Orangeburg in particular has a limited remaining service life regardless of its current condition and is worth flagging even absent an active problem.

How the inspection footage gets used afterward

Footage and a written report are provided after the inspection, useful for getting a second opinion, for insurance documentation if damage is involved, or simply for a homeowner's own records on an older home. For a pre-purchase inspection specifically, this documentation is often exactly what a real estate transaction needs to address a sewer line condition before closing rather than after.

FAQ

Sewer Camera Inspection Questions

Can a camera inspection find a leak, or just clogs?

It's built for drain and sewer line condition, cracks, roots, blockages, rather than a pressurized supply-line leak, which leak detection equipment handles separately.

Is a camera inspection worth it before buying a home?

For a home with an older sewer line, yes. It's a relatively small cost that can reveal a five-figure repair need before it becomes the buyer's problem.

How long does an inspection take?

Usually well under an hour for a standard residential line, with the findings reviewed on site.

Do I need to be home during a camera inspection?

Not necessarily for the inspection itself if there's clear access to the line's cleanout, though being present to review the findings afterward is generally worthwhile.

Can a camera inspection be done on a line under a slab?

Yes, through an available cleanout or access point. The camera doesn't require breaking into the slab itself to inspect a line running beneath it.

Can a camera inspection be requested without committing to any repair?

Yes, a standalone inspection with no repair obligation is a normal, standalone service, useful for a homeowner who just wants to know a line's condition before deciding on anything further.

Can you inspect a line that's already been repaired elsewhere?

Yes, a follow-up inspection to verify a prior repair actually holds is a common and reasonable request.

Does a camera inspection work on pipes with standing water in them?

Modern equipment handles this well, though a completely blocked line may need partial clearing first so the camera can actually pass through and record clear, useful footage of the pipe interior.

Do you provide a written report after every inspection?

Yes, a written summary with findings accompanies the video footage for every inspection performed.
