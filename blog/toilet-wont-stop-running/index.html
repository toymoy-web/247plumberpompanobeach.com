What to Do When Your Toilet Won't Stop Running

A running toilet is one of the most common plumbing complaints, and usually one of the cheapest to fix. Here's how to diagnose it before deciding whether to call someone.

By the Pompano Beach 24/7 Plumbing team · Updated 2026

The most likely cause, by far

A flapper valve that isn't sealing properly against the flush valve seat is responsible for the vast majority of running toilets. The flapper is the rubber or plastic piece at the bottom of the tank that lifts when the toilet is flushed and should drop back down to seal the tank. Over time, it warps, cracks, or accumulates mineral deposits that prevent a full seal, and water slowly leaks from the tank into the bowl continuously.

How to confirm it's actually the flapper

Remove the tank lid and watch what happens after a flush completes. If the flapper visibly doesn't sit flat against the seat, or if you can hear or see water trickling into the bowl a minute or more after flushing with no one touching the toilet, that confirms the flapper is the issue. A dye test, adding a few drops of food coloring to the tank and checking if color appears in the bowl without flushing, is another reliable way to confirm a seal problem.

Other, less common causes worth ruling out

A fill valve that doesn't shut off completely once the tank reaches its target level is the second most common cause, usually recognizable by water continuously trickling into the overflow tube rather than stopping at the right level. A float that's set too high, causing water to constantly spill into the overflow tube, is a related and even simpler fix, sometimes just an adjustment rather than a parts replacement.

Why this is worth fixing promptly, even though it's not an emergency

A continuously running toilet can waste hundreds of gallons of water a day, which shows up clearly on the next water bill even though the toilet itself never overflows or causes visible damage. It's exactly the kind of issue that's easy to ignore because nothing looks wrong, right up until the bill arrives.

When it's worth calling rather than fixing it yourself

A flapper or fill valve replacement is a manageable task for many homeowners comfortable with basic tools, and parts are inexpensive at any hardware store. It's worth calling a professional if the issue persists after replacing the obvious parts, if the toilet is old enough that parts are hard to match, or simply if it's not something you want to deal with yourself.

A note on older toilets and mineral buildup

On a toilet more than 15 years old, mineral deposits inside the fill valve and flapper mechanism can make even a freshly replaced part fail sooner than expected, since the surrounding components are also degraded. If a running toilet keeps recurring even after replacing the obvious parts, the fill valve assembly as a whole, not just the flapper, may need replacement rather than continuing to swap individual pieces.

A note on toilets in guest bathrooms and less-used spaces

A toilet in a guest bathroom or other infrequently used space can develop a running issue that goes unnoticed for a long time simply because no one is around to hear it. Periodically checking less-used bathrooms, even just listening for a minute near the toilet, catches this kind of hidden waste before it accumulates into a genuinely large amount on the water bill.

FAQ

Running Toilet Questions

Can I replace a toilet flapper myself?

Yes, for most standard toilets this is a straightforward task: shut off the water, flush to empty the tank, unhook the old flapper, and attach a matching replacement, widely available at any hardware store.

How do I know if I need a fill valve instead of a flapper?

If water continuously trickles into the overflow tube rather than leaking from the flapper seal specifically, the fill valve is the more likely culprit rather than the flapper.

Is a running toilet ever a sign of a bigger problem?

Rarely. It's almost always a worn, inexpensive part rather than a symptom of anything more serious with the home's plumbing.

How much does a running toilet actually cost in wasted water?

It varies by how badly it's leaking, but hundreds of gallons a day is common for a toilet that's been running unnoticed for a while, which shows up as a real, noticeable jump on the water bill.

Can a running toilet cause it to overflow?

Not typically. A running toilet from a flapper or fill valve issue leaks water continuously but doesn't usually cause overflow, which is a separate issue involving a clog or a stuck float.

Is it normal for a toilet to run briefly after flushing?

Yes, a few seconds of refill sound is completely normal. The concern is water continuing to run or trickle a full minute or more after the tank should have finished refilling.

Can I use a toilet while waiting for a repair part?

Usually yes, a running toilet remains fully functional for flushing. The issue is wasted water, not an inability to use the fixture.

Does toilet paper type affect how well a toilet flushes?

Very absorbent, thick toilet paper can occasionally require more flush power to clear, though this is a minor factor compared to the toilet's own mechanical condition.

Can a toilet's flush handle itself cause running issues?

Yes, a handle that sticks in the down position, or an internal chain that's tangled or too short, can hold the flapper open and cause continuous running separate from the flapper's own condition.
